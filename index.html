Cardiac and anthraquinone glycosides, distilled into five ways to review — rules, structures, quick recall, and self-testing.

Overview

The rules that actually get tested — not the full lecture. Filter by topic.

Glycon & Aglycon

  • Glycoside = Glycon (sugar) + glycosidic linkage + Aglycon (non-sugar).
  • Most common sugar: β-glucose; only the β-form occurs naturally in plants.
  • Chemically, glycosides are acetals — essentially sugar ethers.
  • Glycon → solubility, absorption & distribution (kinetics).
  • Aglycon → responsible for the pharmacological activity.

Linkage Types

  • O-glycoside — most common; sugar linked via oxygen.
  • S-glycoside — sugar linked to sulfur, e.g. sinigrin.
  • N-glycoside — sugar linked to nitrogen, e.g. adenosine.
  • C-glycoside — sugar bound directly to carbon, e.g. aloin.

Stability & Plant Role

  • Hydrolyzed by acids, stable to alkalis; also cleaved by glycosidase enzymes.
  • Emulsin (bitter almond) and Myrosine (black mustard) are classic hydrolyzing enzymes.
  • Plant roles: detoxification, defense, sugar reserve, pigmentation, toxin storage.

Cardiac Genin Structure

  • Steroidal aglycon = cyclopentaphenanthrene nucleus.
  • β-OH at C-3 always carries the glycosidic linkage.
  • β-OH at C-14 → maximal activity, rapid action.
  • Unsaturated 5- or 6-membered lactone ring at C-17, beta configuration.
  • Ring junctions: A/B usually cis, B/C always trans, C/D always cis.

Mechanism & Use

  • Inhibits Na-K ATPase → ↑ intracellular Ca²⁺ → ↑ inotropy, ↓ conduction velocity.
  • Increases cardiac output/work capacity without raising oxygen consumption.
  • Main use: congestive heart failure (CHF). Digoxin is the most widely used drug.
  • Biosynthesis: acetate–mevalonate pathway; pregnenolone +C2 → cardenolide, +C3 → bufadienolide.

Cardenolide vs Bufadienolide

  • Cardenolide = C23, 5-membered lactone (e.g. digitoxigenin).
  • Bufadienolide = C24, 6-membered lactone (e.g. scillarenin).
  • Digitalis purpurea (foxglove): digitoxin, gitoxin, gitaloxin.
  • Digitalis lanata: source of digoxin — more rapidly absorbed than purpurea glycosides.
  • Nerium oleander: oleandrin & neandrin — highly toxic; treat with activated charcoal + supportive care.
  • Squill (Urginea): scillaren A — bufadienolide; red squill is used as rat poison.

Oxidation Levels

  • Anthracene derivatives: anthranol, anthrone, oxanthrone, anthraquinone, dianthrone.
  • Free anthraquinones show little activity — the sugar transports the aglycon to the colon.
  • Anthranol/anthrone glycosides are more irritant/gripping than anthraquinone forms.

Laxative Action

  • ↑ Tone of colon smooth muscle + stimulates water/electrolyte secretion.
  • Effect appears 8–12 hours after oral dosing (colonic bacteria hydrolyze the glycoside).
  • Long-term use → laxative dependence & habitual constipation.
  • Biosynthesis: shikimic acid → 1-naphthol + IPP → cyclization → anthraquinone nucleus.

Key Plants

  • Senna (Cassia acutifolia/angustifolia): Sennosides A/B (major), C/D (minor) — dianthrone O-glycosides.
  • Cascara sagrada (Rhamnus purshiana): Cascarosides A–D; bark aged 1 year to lose emetic principle.
  • Aloe (Aloe perri/barbadensis): Barbaloin (C-glycoside) → aloe-emodin; also used for burns.
  • Rhubarb (Rheum officinale): chrysophanol, rhein, aloe-emodin; stronger gripping effect.

O-glycoside

Sugar linked via an oxygen atom to an alcoholic, phenolic or carboxyl group of the aglycon. The most widely distributed type — e.g. digoxin.

S-glycoside

Sugar is attached to the sulfur atom of the aglycon — e.g. sinigrin (black mustard).

N-glycoside

Sugar is linked to a nitrogen (amino group) of the aglycon — e.g. adenosine, the nucleosides of DNA/RNA.

C-glycoside

Sugar is condensed directly onto a carbon atom of the aglycon, resistant to ordinary hydrolysis — e.g. aloin (barbaloin).
